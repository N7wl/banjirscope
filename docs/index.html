How it works
  1. Radar sees through cloud. NISAR's L-band radar sends its own microwave pulses, so monsoon cloud and night don't matter.
  2. Water is dark. Calm water reflects the pulse away from the satellite like a mirror, so it looks black. Fields, trees and buildings scatter it back and look grey to white.
  3. Same viewpoint, two dates. We compare a dry-season image with a flood-date image taken from the same orbit track, so differences come from the ground, not the viewing angle.
  4. Clean, then compare. A Lee filter removes radar "speckle". Then a log-ratio image (after ÷ before, in dB) highlights where the ground got much darker.
  5. Automatic thresholds. Otsu's method picks the water and change thresholds from the image histograms. A pixel is flooded if it is water now, wasn't before, and darkened strongly.

Limits: flooded forests and towns can look brighter at L-band (the signal bounces off water and walls), so they are missed. Paddies flooded on purpose for planting can be counted as floods.
